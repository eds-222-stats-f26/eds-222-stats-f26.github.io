Background

Lead is elevated in the drip line compared to the rest of the yard.

Neighborhoods north of the Exide plant were exposed to more lead than those south.

These are both claims that we can investigate using statistical inference. In this week’s lab, you’ll reframe the claims using statistical language, use a statistical experiment to quantify the evidence in the sample, and interpret your results responsibly.

Claim Question type Statistical experiment
Lead is elevated in the drip line compared to the rest of the yard. Yes/no? Permutation
Neighborhoods north of the Exide plant were exposed to more lead than those south. How much? Bootstrapping

Calculate the observed value of the statistic

We’ll use the difference in means between samples from the drip line and the yard as the statistic for this question.

Important

Remember, the mean isn’t always the most appropriate statistic for representing a distribution! Always think critically about how your data relates to the question.

Calculate the difference in means between the drip line and yard observations in the sample. Remember, this should be a single number (not a data frame or pair of means).

Shuffle the predictor variable and recalculate the statistic

This statistical experiment simulates the expected variation in our statistic (difference in means) just due to chance alone. To conduct this experiment, we have to break the connection between the predictor variable (location code) and response (lead concentration). Randomizing the predictor leaves the variation in the response untouched, while removing any assocation between the two.

Shuffle the predictor variable and recalculate the statistic. Do it just once - we’ll repeat in the next section.

Tip

sample() can be used to shuffle vectors.

Repeat shuffling

In the previous section, you simulated one statistic from the null hypothesis. What we need now is a whole bunch of statistics from the null hypothesis - our null distribution.

Repeat shuffling 10,000 times to create a vector that simulates the null distribution.

replicate() repeatedly runs expressions over and over.

Investigating “how much?” questions with bootstrapping

Now, let’s investigate the claim “Neighborhoods north of the Exide plant were exposed to more lead than those south” as a how much? question.

Calculate the observed value of the statistic

Once again, let’s use the difference in means as our statistic. Calculate the difference in means between properties in the extended north area and those in the extended south area.

Tip

Use dplyr functions to wrangle the soil core data for this part. Properties starting with “ENA” are in the extended north area, and the “ESA” prefix means extended south area. str_sub() will help here.

Resample observations and recalculate the statistic

Bootstrapping simulates the expected variation in our statistic (difference in means) from sampling. Compare this to permutation, which is about variation just due to chance. For this experiment, we will assume the sample is representative of the population and repeatedly draw “new” samples. This process simulates the process of collecting new samples.

Resample rows from the data frame and recalculate the statistic. Repeat this 10,000 times to simulate the sampling distribution.

Tip

Use slice_sample() with replacement to get bootstrap samples.

Visualize the sampling distribution using a histogram.

Find the confidence interval

The previous figure showed how your statistic varies due to slight differences between samples. Use this sampling distribution to estimate the 95% confidence interval.

Note

A confidence interval is a range of values that you are confident contains the population parameter.

Tip

Use the quantile() function to find the middle 95% of the sampling distribution.

Plot the sampling distribution again, but this time add vertical lines indicating the observed statistic and the 95% CI.

Interpret your results

Recap

In this lab, you investigated two claims using statistical experiments to evaluate evidence.

From this exercise, the practical information you should remember:

“yes/no” questions “how much?” questions
Inference target P-value Confidence interval
Statistical experiment Permutation Bootstrapping
Simulate a… Null distribution Sampling distribution
Centered on… 0 The observed statistic
Randomize with… sample() (no replacement) slice_sample() (with replacement)
Simulate repeatedly replicate() replicate()

More importantly, remember how to use these tools responsibly. A p-value and a confidence interval are tools for checking our own cognitive biases. They are useful lines of evidence, but no one statistical procedure ever tells the whole story.

Always think critically!
